Nine million weekly downloads, no budget, and a triage process that finally made the backlog shrink.
Maintaining a popular library with two volunteers
Our date parsing library started as a weekend project and is now a dependency of thousands of applications. For most of its life it has been maintained by two people with full-time jobs. Last spring the issue tracker passed 900 open issues, and we realised that answering them one by one was never going to work.
Triage as a weekly ritual
We now spend one hour every Tuesday on triage and nothing else. Every new issue gets a label within a week: bug, feature, question or duplicate. Questions are redirected to the discussion forum, where other users answer most of them faster than we could.
Feature requests are closed with a polite note unless they come with a design proposal. That rule felt harsh at first, but it turned vague wishes into concrete discussions, and several contributors wrote excellent proposals once they knew what we needed.
gh issue list --label needs-triage --limit 50 --json number,title,createdAt \ | jq -r '.[] | "\(.number)\t\(.createdAt[:10])\t\(.title)"'
Saying no to breaking changes
A library used this widely cannot change behaviour casually. We keep a public compatibility policy that promises no breaking changes outside major versions and at least twelve months of deprecation warnings before anything is removed.
Funding changed less than expected
Two companies now sponsor the project. The money pays for a security audit every year and for occasional contractor time on large refactors. It did not buy more maintainer hours, because time, not money, was always the scarce resource.
The backlog is down to 210 open issues. More importantly, neither of us dreads opening the tracker any more, which is the metric that decides whether a volunteer project survives.
