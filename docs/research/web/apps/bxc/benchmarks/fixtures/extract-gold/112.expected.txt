Fees look tiny on a statement. Over thirty years they are not.
The quiet maths behind a boring index fund
Suppose two investors each put 500 a month into funds that track the same market index for thirty years. The only difference is the annual fee: one pays 0.1 percent, the other pays 1.2 percent. Before fees, both portfolios earn the same return. After thirty years, the gap between them is large enough to buy a small car.
Why a one percent difference compounds
A fee is charged on the whole balance every year, not just on the money you added. As the balance grows, the same percentage takes a larger amount, and that amount is no longer invested, so it stops earning returns for every remaining year.
Assuming a six percent annual return before costs, the low-fee investor ends up with roughly 490,000 while the high-fee investor ends up with about 400,000. Nearly ninety thousand has gone to fees and to the growth those fees would have produced.
Annual fee Balance after 30 years Lost to costs
0.10% 490,000 8,400
0.50% 455,000 43,000
1.20% 400,000 98,000
What an index fund does not promise
Low fees do not protect against falling markets. An index fund will fall exactly as far as the market it tracks, and investors who sell in a panic lock in those losses. The case for index funds rests on the idea that most active managers fail to beat the index after costs over long periods, not on any claim of safety.
