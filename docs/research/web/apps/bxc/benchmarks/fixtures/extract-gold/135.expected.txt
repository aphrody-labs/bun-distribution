Rotating TLS certificates without waking anyone up
How we moved from calendar reminders to fully automated renewal across 340 services.
Two years ago our certificate inventory lived in a spreadsheet with a column called expiry and a column called owner. The owner column was mostly empty. We had three outages in eighteen months caused by expired certificates, and each one was found by a customer before it was found by us.
This post describes the boring, reliable system that replaced the spreadsheet, and the mistakes we made along the way.
Step one: find every certificate
You cannot automate what you cannot see. We started with a scanner that connected to every listening port inside our network ranges and recorded the certificate chain it received. The first run found 1,112 certificates, roughly three times the number in the spreadsheet.
About a third were internal test services nobody used. Another third were duplicates served by different load balancers. The remaining 340 became the scope of the project.
Step two: short lifetimes on purpose
It sounds backwards, but the most important decision was to shorten certificate lifetimes from one year to thirty days. A certificate that expires every month forces the renewal path to work every month. A certificate that expires every year lets a broken renewal path hide for eleven months.
issuer: internal-acme renew_before: 10d lifetime: 30d reload: strategy: signal signal: SIGHUP alerts: - when: days_remaining < 7 notify: owning-team
Step three: reload without restarts
Renewal is easy. Getting a running process to pick up the new certificate is where most automation breaks. Some servers reload on a signal, some watch the file, and a few legacy applications only read certificates at startup.
For that last group we put a small sidecar proxy in front of the application and let the proxy terminate TLS. The application never touches a certificate again, and the proxy reloads cleanly.
Reload method Services Median reload time
Signal (SIGHUP) 181 40 ms
File watch 97 1.2 s
Sidecar proxy 62 15 ms
What we would do differently
We alerted on certificates expiring within seven days, which was too late for the first month while teams were still learning the system. Starting with a twenty-day threshold and tightening it later would have avoided a lot of late-night messages.
Since the rollout finished we have had zero expiry incidents. The spreadsheet has been archived, and the owner column finally has an answer: the automation owns it.
