I ran my first marathon at forty-three, after a sports physiologist told me that the plan I had downloaded was written for a twenty-five-year-old with nothing else to do. She was right. The plan assumed I could run six days a week and recover overnight. I could run four, and recovery took two.
Fewer runs, each with a purpose
We rebuilt the plan around four runs: one long run at an easy pace, one session of intervals, one steady run at goal marathon pace and one short recovery jog. Everything else became cycling, swimming or strength work, which kept my aerobic fitness growing without the pounding.
Long run: 90 minutes to 3 hours at conversational pace
Intervals: 6 to 8 repeats of 800 metres
Marathon-pace run: 10 to 16 kilometres
Recovery: 30 minutes, slower than feels necessary
Strength work is not optional
Muscle mass declines with age, and the tendons around the knee and ankle take longer to adapt to load. Two short strength sessions a week, built around squats, lunges, calf raises and single-leg balance work, did more to prevent injury than any pair of shoes.
The physiologist also insisted on sleep. When I tracked it, my worst training weeks lined up almost perfectly with the weeks I slept less than seven hours a night.
You do not get fitter during the run. You get fitter while you recover from it.
I finished in four hours and eleven minutes, which will not impress anyone at my running club. I finished uninjured and signed up for a second race a week later, which impressed me.
Training for a first marathon after forty
Recovery, not mileage, turned out to be the limiting factor.
