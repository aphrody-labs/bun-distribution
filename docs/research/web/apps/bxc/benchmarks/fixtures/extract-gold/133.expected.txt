Why the northern tram extension took eleven years to approve
A line that looked simple on a napkin ran into soil surveys, heritage bridges and a very stubborn budget committee.
When the first sketch of the northern tram extension was presented to the council in 2014, it fit on a single slide. Four kilometres of track, seven stops, a depot on disused rail land. Planners promised trams running by the end of the decade, and nobody in the room seriously questioned the timeline.
Eleven years later the first rails are only now being welded, and the story of that delay says less about incompetence than about how many small, reasonable objections a transport project has to absorb before it can break ground.
The ground was not what the maps said
The original alignment followed an old canal towpath. It was cheap land, already flat, and it avoided demolishing houses. The trouble started when geotechnical engineers drilled test holes in 2016 and found a layer of soft peat almost six metres deep under a third of the route.
Building on peat is possible, but it means piling, and piling means vibration. Residents of the Victorian terraces along the canal commissioned their own survey, which concluded that the foundations of forty houses could crack. The council paid for a second opinion, then a third.
Every report was technically correct. They simply answered different questions, and each one added a year.
Heritage and the swing bridge
The second obstacle was a cast-iron swing bridge from 1887 that the tram needed to cross. Listed structures cannot be modified without consent from the national heritage body, and the bridge could not carry the weight of a loaded tram without strengthening its deck.
Engineers proposed a new concrete span beside the old one. Heritage officers rejected the first design as visually intrusive, accepted a slimmer steel design two years later, and asked for the original bridge to be restored at the project's expense as a condition of approval.
What finally unlocked the money
Cost estimates climbed from 180 million to 312 million over the decade, mostly because of inflation in construction materials and the extra piling. The regional government refused to cover the gap until the council agreed to a fixed-price contract with penalties for late delivery.
The breakthrough came from an unexpected source: a housing developer planning 2,400 homes near the depot site agreed to fund two of the seven stops in exchange for a faster planning decision on its own scheme.
Regional transport grant: 140 million
Municipal bond issued in 2023: 96 million
Developer contribution for two stops: 38 million
European infrastructure fund: 38 million
Services are now expected to start in the spring of 2029. The council has published a monthly progress dashboard, partly to rebuild trust and partly, officials admit, so that the next delay is explained before rumours explain it for them.
