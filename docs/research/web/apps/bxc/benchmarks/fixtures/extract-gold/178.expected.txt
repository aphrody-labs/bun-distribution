Twelve metres below the surface, lines of rope strung between metal frames are covered in fragments of coral no longer than a finger. In eight months each fragment will have grown large enough to be cemented onto a patch of reef that was flattened by a typhoon and then bleached by a marine heatwave.
The nursery is one of several run by a university marine station, and it is part of a wider effort to find out whether active restoration can keep up with the pace of damage that warming seas are causing.
Why ropes instead of the seabed
Fragments placed directly on the seabed are often buried by sediment or eaten by snails and fish. Suspended on ropes, they receive more light and more water flow, and divers can clean algae off them quickly. Survival rates in the rope nursery have averaged 83 percent, compared with about 50 percent on the seabed.
Collect naturally broken fragments after storms
Tie fragments to ropes with biodegradable ties
Clean the lines every two weeks
Transplant when colonies reach 10 to 15 centimetres
Choosing heat-tolerant corals
The researchers deliberately collect fragments from colonies that survived the last bleaching event. The hope is that these colonies carry some natural tolerance to heat, so the restored reef will be better prepared for the next heatwave than the one it replaces.
It is not a guarantee. Tolerance can depend on the algae living inside the coral as much as on the coral itself, and a heatwave that is severe enough will bleach even the toughest colonies.
We are not trying to rebuild the reef of 1980. We are trying to build the reef that can survive 2040.
The team measures success by fish, not by coral alone. Surveys on the first restored plot recorded twice as many fish species after two years as on an untouched plot nearby, which suggests the transplanted colonies are already providing shelter.
