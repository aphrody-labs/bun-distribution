For most of the twentieth century a posting to a rock lighthouse meant a month on and a month off, three keepers sharing a tower barely wider than a kitchen, and a supply boat that came when the weather allowed rather than when the calendar said it should.
By 1998 every lighthouse on the coast had been automated. The lamps still turn, but they are monitored from an office on the mainland, and the keepers who once polished the lenses by hand are now in their seventies and eighties.
A routine measured in watches
Keepers worked in four-hour watches through the night. The lamp had to be lit at sunset, the clockwork that rotated the lens had to be wound every half hour on older stations, and the fog signal had to be started the moment visibility fell below a set distance.
Daytime brought maintenance: cleaning soot from the lantern glass, painting railings that the salt air attacked constantly, recording weather observations every three hours for the meteorological service.
You learned to sleep through the foghorn and wake up the second it stopped. Silence was the alarm.
Why automation came so quickly
Reliable electric lamps, solar panels and radio telemetry made unattended operation possible by the 1970s, but the real driver was cost. Keeping three people on a rock, relieving them by helicopter and paying for their shore housing was far more expensive than a remote monitoring system.
Some keepers welcomed the change after years of isolation. Others argued that no sensor could replace a person watching for ships in trouble, and pointed to rescues that had started with a keeper noticing a light where no light should be.
Several former stations are now holiday cottages, and a few host small museums staffed by volunteers. At one of them, a retired keeper still gives tours on summer Saturdays, pointing out the scratch marks on the stairs where the lamp oil cans were dragged up for decades.
The last lighthouse keepers of the western isles
Automation ended a two-hundred-year profession in a single decade. The people who lived it remember the silence most.
