The starter was never the problem. The kitchen was.
What three months of failed sourdough taught me about temperature
I followed the recipe exactly, which is the first thing everybody says right before admitting they did not. In my case the flour, the water and the timings were correct. What I ignored was the one number most recipes mention only in passing: the temperature of the dough.
My kitchen in winter sits at around 17 degrees. The recipe I used was written by someone baking at 24. That seven-degree gap roughly doubles the time fermentation needs, so every loaf I shaped was underproofed, dense at the bottom and torn at the top.
Measuring instead of guessing
The fix cost less than a bag of good flour. A cheap probe thermometer told me that my dough came off the mixing bowl at 19 degrees and cooled from there. Once I started using water at 30 degrees, the dough began bulk fermentation at a steady 25.
Professional bakers use a formula called the desired dough temperature. You add up the temperature of the room, the flour and the starter, subtract that from a target multiplied by three, and the result is the water temperature you need. It sounds fussy, but it takes thirty seconds.
water_temp = (target_dough_temp * 3) - (room_temp + flour_temp + starter_temp) example: (25 * 3) - (17 + 16 + 21) = 21 degrees
Reading the dough, not the clock
Temperature control made the timings predictable, but the bigger change was learning to watch the dough itself. A properly fermented dough has grown by about half, has a slightly domed surface, jiggles when you shake the container and shows bubbles along the sides.
I now mark the level of the dough on the container with a strip of tape at the start of bulk fermentation. It is the least romantic tool in my kitchen and the one that improved my bread the most.
Volume increase of 50 to 75 percent
Domed, slightly glossy surface
Visible bubbles on the sides and bottom
A wobble like set jelly when the tub is moved
My last ten loaves have been consistent enough that I stopped photographing them. That, more than any crumb shot, feels like success.
